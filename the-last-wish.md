# *The Last Wish*

Andrzej Sapkowski

## 1. Monsters and Monstrosities

### Context

- **Story:** “The Edge of the World”
- **Speaker:** Geralt
- **Addressed to:** Dandelion

### Passage

> People like to invent monsters and monstrosities. Then they seem less monstrous themselves. When they get blind drunk, cheat, steal, beat their wives, starve an old woman, when they kill a trapped fox with an axe or riddle the last existing unicorn with arrows, they like to think that the bane entering cottages at daybreak is more monstrous than they are. They feel better then. They find it easier to live.
>
> — Geralt

### Meaning

People imagine external monsters so they can avoid confronting their own cruelty. If something else appears more evil, they feel less guilty about what they have done.

### English Notes

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **invent monsters and monstrosities** | imagine or create frightening creatures |
| **seem less monstrous themselves** | appear less cruel or evil by comparison |
| **blind drunk** | extremely drunk |
| **starve someone** | deprive someone of food or allow them to go hungry |
| **a trapped fox** | a fox caught in a trap |
| **riddle something with arrows** | pierce something with many arrows |
| **bane** | a cause of death, destruction, or suffering; here, a legendary monster |
| **at daybreak** | at dawn, when daylight begins |
| **more monstrous than they are** | more cruel or horrible than those people |
| **find it easier to live** | feel more able to live with their own actions |

### Grammar

- **seem + adjective:** *They seem less monstrous.*
- **themselves** emphasizes that the people, not just their actions, appear less monstrous.
- **When they get..., cheat, steal...** uses parallel verbs after the same subject, **they**.
- **entering cottages at daybreak** is a participial phrase modifying **the bane**.
- **more + adjective + than:** *The bane is more monstrous than they are.*
- **find + it + adjective + to do:** *They find it easier to live.*

### Modern Paraphrase

> People invent monsters to make themselves feel less monstrous. By imagining something worse than themselves, they find it easier to live with their own cruelty.

## 2. Agriculture and Poetry

### Context

- **Story:** “The Edge of the World”
- **Dialogue between:** Dandelion and Geralt

### Passage

> **Dandelion:** “It is hard to believe that this should be the edge of the world, the edge of civilization. Just look, Geralt. Rye like gold, and a mounted peasant could hide in that corn. Or that oilseed—look, how enormous.”
>
> **Geralt:** “You know about agriculture?”
>
> **Dandelion:** “We poets have to know about everything. Otherwise we'd compromise our work. One has to learn, my dear fellow, learn. The fate of the world depends on agriculture, so it's good to know about it. Agriculture feeds, clothes, protects from the cold, provides entertainment and supports art.”

### Meaning

Dandelion admires the rich farmland and jokes that poets must understand everything. He argues that agriculture sustains life and even makes entertainment and art possible.

### English Notes

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **It is hard to believe that this should be...** | literary expression of surprise: “It is hard to believe that this is...” |
| **the edge of civilization** | the outer limit of the settled or developed world |
| **Rye like gold** | an elliptical description: “The rye is like gold.” |
| **a mounted peasant** | a farmer riding a horse |
| **hide in that corn** | be concealed by the tall crop; **corn** here means grain or cereal crops, not specifically maize |
| **oilseed** | a crop grown for producing oil |
| **compromise our work** | weaken the quality or integrity of our work |
| **One has to learn** | a formal, impersonal way to say “You have to learn.” |
| **the fate of the world depends on...** | the future or survival of the world relies on... |
| **clothe** | provide someone with clothing |
| **support art** | make artistic work possible through resources or funding |

### Grammar

- **should** can express surprise in literary British English: *It is strange that he should say that.*
- **Rye like gold** omits **is** for a vivid, compact description.
- **Otherwise + would:** *We have to learn. Otherwise, we'd compromise our work.*
- **depend on + noun:** *The fate of the world depends on agriculture.*
- **feeds, clothes, protects, provides, supports** are parallel present-tense verbs with the same subject, **agriculture**.
- The objects are partly understood: agriculture **feeds us, clothes us, and protects us** from the cold.

### Modern Paraphrase

> “It is hard to believe that this is the edge of civilization. Look at the golden rye—the crops are tall enough to hide a person on horseback.”
>
> “You know about farming?”
>
> “Poets need to know about everything, or our work will suffer. Agriculture feeds and clothes us, protects us from the cold, and makes art and entertainment possible.”

## 3. An Irresistible Nonexistent Thing

### Context

- **Story:** “The Edge of the World”
- **Speaker:** Geralt

### Passage

> I could never resist the temptation of having a look at something that doesn't exist.

### Why It Works

The sentence is deliberately paradoxical: if the creature does not exist, there should be nothing to see. Geralt uses that contradiction to express curiosity and dry humor.

### Reusable Pattern

> **I could never resist the temptation to + verb.**

- **I could never resist the temptation to investigate a mystery.**
- **I could never resist the temptation to open a forbidden door.**
- **I could never resist the temptation to see something that supposedly doesn't exist.**

The original **the temptation of having a look** is correct. In modern English, **the temptation to have a look** is more common.
